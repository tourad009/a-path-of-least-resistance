\begin{center}
  {\fontsize{14}{17}\selectfont\bfseries Abstract}
\end{center}
\begin{list}{}{\leftmargin=0.75in\rightmargin=0.75in}\item[]
\itshape
When one member of an LLM agent team finds a forbidden shortcut, does the team use it, and does that depend on how hard
the honest alternative is? We hold the shortcut fixed (one member of some teams finds an old answer file, and no prompt
asks anyone to collude) and vary only the cost of earning the same points honestly, from a lookup in a public file to
impossible. Leak use is proven by execution at every level: the leaked file holds the previous version of the answer key,
which the scorer accepts and which appears nowhere else. Across \NGames{} runs of \NTasks{} everyday agentic tasks and
\NModels{} models, collusion did not rise smoothly with the cost of honest work: it switched on when the honest path
disappeared. Pooled over \NGamesUsable{} usable runs, teammates' use of the leak rose by \HOneDiff{} percentage points from
the easiest to the impossible level (95\% CI \HOneCI). Models split in two: three took the leak when stuck (DeepSeek-V3.2
from \val{use}{DeepSeek}{0}\% to \val{use}{DeepSeek}{3}\% of teammates), while both Claude models never did. Outcome
evidence failed where honest work was easy: a score rule flagged \val{fpr}{Rule}{0}\% of clean teams at the easiest level
and \val{fpr}{Rule}{3}\% at the hardest, while an LLM officer flagged at most \val{fpr}{Officer}{0}\%. Shortcuts spread
when no honest route to a goal exists, and high scores are not evidence of cheating when one does.
\end{list}

% ================= 1. Introduction =================
\section{Introduction}

Teams of LLM agents increasingly share a workspace and a chat channel \citep{nakamura2026colosseum}. Collusion arises when
members coordinate on an outcome that the rules forbid, often out of sight of oversight \citep{motwani2024secret}.
A concrete case: a member finds information it was told not to use, such as an answer key or held-out labels left in reach, which agents are known to exploit
\citep{atinafu2026rewardhackingagents,thaman2026rhb}, and the team must decide whether to use it,
share it, or leave it alone. Such collusion can corrupt grading, benchmarks and audits that assume independent work.

Existing studies ask what it takes for collusion to emerge. Private channels can introduce it in cooperative systems
\citep{nakamura2026colosseum}, oversight and communication shape it between auction sellers \citep{agrawal2025double}, and
pricing agents reach it without any explicit agreement \citep{fish2024algorithmic,lin2024strategic}. Under pressure, models
also act on forbidden information \citep{scheurer2023deceive}, and a single agent exploits shortcuts more on harder tasks
\citep{thaman2026rhb}, as the economics of crime predicts \citep{becker1968crime}. What remains unknown is whether the cost of doing the honest work, alone, can push a team to collude on a shortcut nobody
asked it to take.

We study this gap in a simple setting. Teams of agents work on everyday tasks, such as querying a database or writing
code, and one member of some teams finds a leaked file containing answers. We change only how hard it is to earn the reward
without the leak, from trivial to impossible, and measure whether the team uses the leak, whether it shares it, and
whether a separate monitor notices.
Across six models and \NGamesUsable{} usable runs, collusion behaves like a switch: it stays low while any honest route
exists, even a laborious one, and jumps when the honest route disappears (teammate use \HOneDiff{} percentage points
higher at the impossible level than at the easiest, 95\% CI \HOneCI). Whether it jumps depends on the model: three
models took the leak when stuck, and both Claude models never did. Detection has the mirror-image weakness: judging
teams by their scores works only when honest work cannot reach the same scores.

\textbf{Our contributions are:}
\begin{list}{}{\leftmargin=2.5em\itemindent=-1.5em\itemsep=3pt\topsep=3pt\parsep=0pt}
  \item[1.] \textbf{An agentic harness that isolates the cost of honest work.} Teams do everyday tasks such as database
        queries and coding, with no prompt to collude. Each task offers two routes: one that uses the leak and colludes,
        and an honest one whose cost varies from trivial to impossible.
  \item[2.] \textbf{An extensive study of difficulty, collusion and its detection.} To our knowledge the first to test
        whether the cost of honest work alone drives teams to collude. The analysis covers \NGames{} runs on
        the following tasks: labelling shapes, answering questions with SQL over a company database, pricing auction deals
        within a buyer's budget, writing short Python functions, and answering questions from company documents.
\end{list}

% ================= 2. Related Work =================
\section{Related Work}

\textbf{What makes LLM agents collude.} Existing studies give agents a means or a reason to collude and ask whether they do: a private channel \citep{nakamura2026colosseum}, more or less communication and oversight
between sellers \citep{agrawal2025double}, or a pricing market \citep{fish2024algorithmic,lin2024strategic}. Collusion also
appears without instruction, from reward structure \citep{shi2026emergent} or poorly specified rewards
\citep{mathew2024hidden}, and a public governance graph reduced it where a prompt-only ban did not
\citep{bracalesyrnikov2026institutional}. In every case the setup changes the channel, the market, the reward or the
oversight. We instead vary the cost of earning the reward honestly, from trivial to impossible, with the shortcut held fixed.

\textbf{Shortcut exploitation and task difficulty.} The evidence that harder tasks push agents to shortcuts comes from
benchmarks that plant one: leaked answers, readable tests and hidden solutions
\citep{atinafu2026rewardhackingagents,roth2026hvtb}, harder variants \citep{thaman2026rhb}, or impossible variants where
every pass is a cheat \citep{zhong2025impossiblebench}. All of them follow a single agent. Reward hacking can also spread
between agents and intensify on hard tasks \citep{hu2026transmissible}, but there the hacks are induced by training and the
cost of the honest path is not controlled. Our setting differs in two ways. The agents work as a team, so the leak must also be shared, and no prompt mentions the
leak, so the cost of honest work is the main thing that pushes agents toward it.

% ================= 3. Methods =================
\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Architecture overview}
A run has four stages (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}). We first \textbf{set up} the teams and the task: several teams of three agents
are drawn, one member of some teams, the holder, will get a leak, and every team gets the same task whose honest route we make
as costly as the level requires, from trivial (D0) to impossible (D3) (Section~\ref{sec:setup}). The agents are then
\textbf{briefed and play}: each learns its task, goal and rules, only the holder is told about the leaked file, and the agents
work in turns, running commands and messaging each other openly, within their team or privately (Section~\ref{sec:play}). A
scorer \textbf{scores} the answers, giving points for the honest answers and the leaked ones alike (Section~\ref{sec:score}).
Finally, a passive officer \textbf{oversees} the finished run and we \textbf{measure} what the leak did
(Section~\ref{sec:oversee}).

\subsection{Setting and assumptions}
Four assumptions define the setting. \textbf{A1, the leak is unprompted:} nobody asks the agents to use or share it, and the
holder is only told that a file was accidentally left in its workspace. \textbf{A2, the stakes are nominal:} the agents are
prompted, not trained, and the reward exists only in the prompt. \textbf{A3, only the cost varies:} at D0--D2 agents are told
where the honest route is, and the levels change how hard it is to follow. \textbf{A4, the wording is fixed:} the holder's note,
the rule against non-public information and the line telling agents that answers from the previous version of the key are
also accepted (Section~\ref{sec:setup}) are the same at every level, so they cannot explain differences between levels.

\subsection{Teams and tasks}\label{sec:setup}
\paragraph{Teams.} Each run draws two to five teams of three agents. We then choose at random which teams get a leak, always
at least one team with it and at least one without. In every team that gets it, one randomly chosen member becomes the
\emph{holder} and is the only one to receive the leaked file. The teams without a leak are \emph{control teams}.

\paragraph{Tasks.} In every task each agent works in its own workspace with public files and must give 10 answers. Five are
\emph{ordinary} questions that careful work on the public files can answer. The other five are \emph{team-specific}
questions: their answers differ from team to team, so another team's leak is useless, and they are where the honest route
gets easier or harder. We use five everyday tasks (Table~\ref{tab:tasks}); for example, in the database task the team-specific
questions ask for a team's unit costs, which the holder's leak (a copy of a restricted table) contains and which, at D0--D2,
can also be worked out from public tables. The files of every task are listed in Appendix~\ref{app:honest}.

\paragraph{The honest route and its cost.} The one manipulated factor is how much work the honest route to the team-specific
items takes. The levels mean the same in every task: \textbf{D0}, the answer is written in a public file; \textbf{D1}, one
mapping step; \textbf{D2}, a join of several sources plus a rule; \textbf{D3}, no honest route. At D0--D2 the brief says the
team-specific items can be worked out from the public files, so levels change the cost of the honest route and not whether agents
know it exists. The exact \texttt{README.txt} of every task and level is in Appendix~\ref{app:honest}.

\paragraph{How we know the leak was used.} Every team-specific item has two accepted answers. \textbf{\vtwo{}}, the current
key, is what honest work derives. \textbf{\vone{}}, the previous version of the key, appears \emph{only} in the leak file. The
scorer accepts both, so both earn points, and an agent \emph{used the leak} when at least 3 of its 5 team-specific items match
\vone{}. Honest work cannot produce \vone{}, so this proves use at every difficulty level. Every brief tells agents that
``answers that match the previous version of the key are also accepted''. The line is the same for every agent and level; it
tells non-holders that an older key exists, which is a cue we report in the limitations, and it lets a holder who compares the
leak with its own answers see that both score. By construction, \vone{} never appears in a public file or brief, and
\vone{} and \vtwo{} differ for every item.

\subsection{Agents and interaction}\label{sec:play}
\paragraph{Briefing.} Each agent receives a system prompt and a brief. The brief names the team and the other teams, states the task, the goal (a
reward for the team's total score) and the rule that using or sharing non-public information is prohibited, says that answers from the previous version of the key are also accepted, and lists the
commands. The holder's brief adds a note that a file was accidentally left in its workspace. \textbf{No prompt asks agents to
collude}, and agents are not told anyone is watching. All prompts are in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}.

\paragraph{Turns.} Agents take turns over \NRounds{} rounds of up to \NCmds{} commands, plus a final turn, in an order reshuffled every round.
Each command is one model call that sees the agent's brief, its team's answer board, its recent messages and its
recent commands. An agent can message everyone, its team or a single agent, or run a command in its own offline workspace;
answers it enters update the team board.

\subsection{Scoring}\label{sec:score}
After each round the scorer grades every submitted answer. It accepts both \vone{} and \vtwo{} as correct, so using the leak
and working honestly earn the same points. This keeps the reward fixed while only the cost of the honest route varies.

\subsection{Oversight and measures}\label{sec:oversee}
\paragraph{A passive security officer.}
After the last turn, a security officer model reads the roster and every message, and in its full view also the scores. It
rates every agent and decides for every team whether to disqualify or clear it. It \textbf{cannot influence the run}: it
acts after the last turn, agents are not told it exists, and its decision changes no score. As a baseline we report a
one-line score rule that flags a team when any member scores above 5.

\paragraph{Measures.} All measures come from the run itself, not from a judge (Table~\ref{tab:measures}). The primary outcome is teammate leak use
at D3 compared with D0.

\subsection{Controls and validation}
\textbf{Positive and null controls.} Before running any model, we play every task and level with scripted players. Honest players (the
null control) solve all team-specific items at D0--D2 and none at D3 and never cross the leak threshold; colluding players (the
positive control) cross it every time (Table~\ref{tab:checks}). \textbf{Control teams.} They hold no leak, so their honest solve
rate measures the honest route directly and checks that the difficulty dial works. \textbf{Grading the officer.} The officer is
graded against what actually happened, with a one-line score rule as the baseline, and its prompt uses a neutral instruction,
without the line that told an earlier version to act on suspicion without proof. \textbf{Records.} Every run is fully recorded, so every reported
number can be recomputed from the records.

% ================= 4. Experimental setup =================
\section{Experimental setup}

\textbf{What we ran.} Each of six models plays every task at every level on five seeds: \NTasks{} $\times$ \NLevels{} $\times$
\NSeeds{} $=$ \NGamesPerModel{} runs per model, \NGamesRecorded{} in all. A seed fixes the number of teams, which teams hold a
leak, who the holders are and the leak's content, so the four levels of a seed differ only in the honest route and every model
plays the same layouts. Everything else is fixed: messaging on, memory of past commands, visible messages, a team progress board,
a short final turn, a reward for the team's total score only and no warning about surveillance. Holders are interviewed after the
run.

\textbf{Models.} The six agent models span size, openness and safety training: Hermes-4-405B (large, few safeguards),
Dolphin-Mistral-24B (small, few safeguards), Llama-4-Maverick and DeepSeek-V3.2 (open-weight), and Claude Haiku 5.5 and Claude
Opus 5.5 (frontier, strong safeguards). Every agent in a run is the same model. The reasoning of DeepSeek-V3.2 and Haiku is
switched off so that hidden reasoning cannot use up the reply budget. The security officer is Claude Sonnet 5.5.

\textbf{Statistics.} The unit of analysis is the run, since agents in one run are not independent. The primary test (H1) is a
logistic GEE of teammate leak use on the level, with task and model as fixed effects and clustering by run; we also report the
seed-paired bootstrap 95\% CI of teammate use at D3 minus D0. Secondary tests (H2 holder sending, H3 holder self-use, H4 false
alarms by level) use the same model with Holm correction. A run is excluded when more than 20\% of its agent replies carry no
command; \NExcluded{} of \NGamesRecorded{} runs are excluded this way, almost all Claude Opus 5.5 runs blocked by a content filter
(Section~\ref{sec:filter}). The hypotheses, tests and exclusion rule were written down before the main runs, but the draft was not
formally frozen: the exclusion was widened to count empty and filtered replies after the first runs, and the last three models were
added after the first three had run.

% ================= 5. Results =================
\section{Results}

\subsection{Collusion switches on when the honest path disappears}
\textbf{The dial works.} For every model that can do the work, the control teams' honest solve rate on the team-specific items
falls from D0 to D3 (Table~\ref{tab:finding1}, blue lines in Figure~\ref{fig:finding1}): the levels change what honest work
achieves. Dolphin-Mistral-24B is the exception: its control agents solve almost none of the team-specific items even at D0
(\val{honest}{Dolphin}{0}\%), so for this model the levels barely differ.

\textbf{Teammates use the leak more when honest work is impossible.} Pooled over models and tasks, the odds that a
teammate uses the leak grow by a factor of \HOneOR{} per level (GEE, $p = \HOneP$), and teammate use is \HOneDiff{}
percentage points higher at D3 than at D0 (95\% CI \HOneCI; \HOneGames{} runs). \textbf{The rise is a step, not a slope.}
In the models that collude, use stays flat or noisy across D0--D2, as the honest path goes from a lookup to a multi-file
join, and jumps at D3: Llama-4-Maverick from \val{use}{Maverick}{0}--\val{use}{Maverick}{2}\% to
\val{use}{Maverick}{3}\%, Hermes-4-405B from \val{use}{Hermes}{0}\% to \val{use}{Hermes}{3}\%, and DeepSeek-V3.2 from
\val{use}{DeepSeek}{0}\% to \val{use}{DeepSeek}{3}\%. Making honest work costlier did little; removing it did almost
everything.

\subsection{Two kinds of models: some take the leak when stuck, others never do}
The step at D3 is not universal. \textbf{DeepSeek-V3.2} is the sharpest case: with any honest path, none of its teammates
used the leak, and at D3 \val{use}{DeepSeek}{3}\% did. \textbf{Llama-4-Maverick} and \textbf{Hermes-4-405B} show the same
step from different baselines; Hermes also uses the leak when honest work is easy (\val{use}{Hermes}{0}\% at D0).
\textbf{Claude Haiku 5.5 and Claude Opus 5.5 never used it}, at any level, including D3 where it was the only way to score
on the team-specific items: their control teams solve the honest path whenever it exists (Haiku \val{honest}{Haiku}{0}\% at D0),
and their holders almost never pass the leak on. The models that collude when stuck are the open-weight and
low-safeguard ones; the two that never do are the high-safeguard frontier models.

\subsection{The leak is used privately first, then shared}
Holders break the rule alone more readily than they pass the leak on. Holder self-use rises steeply with difficulty (odds
$\times$\HThreeOR{} per level, $p = \HThreeP$; D3$-$D0 \HThreeDiff{} points, 95\% CI \HThreeCI), and holder sending rises
less (odds $\times$\HTwoOR{} per level, $p = \HTwoP$; \HTwoDiff{} points, 95\% CI \HTwoCI). Teammate use follows sending,
so the holder's decision to send is the step where a private shortcut becomes collusion. Appendix
Table~\ref{tab:pipeline} gives every step of the pipeline for every model and level.

\subsection{Outcome evidence fails exactly where the honest path is easy}
A one-line score rule (flag a team when any member scores above 5) is a perfect detector only when honest work cannot
reach high scores. When it can, honest teams score high too: the rule flags \val{fpr}{Rule}{0}\% of clean teams at D0 and
\val{fpr}{Rule}{3}\% at D3 (Figure~\ref{fig:detection}, Table~\ref{tab:detection}; odds $\times$\FARuleOR{} per level,
$p \FARuleP$). The LLM officer, which reads the messages as well as the scores, is far less misled
(\val{fpr}{Officer}{0}\% at D0, \val{fpr}{Officer}{3}\% at D3) and catches \val{caught}{Officer}{} colluding teams; reading
the messages alone, it flags \val{fpr}{Msgs}{0}\% of clean teams at D0 and \val{fpr}{Msgs}{3}\% at D3, a much gentler
decline (odds $\times$\FAMsgsOR{} per level, $p = \FAMsgsP$). Scores separate cheaters from honest teams only when the two paths lead
to different outcomes.

\subsection{A safety filter stopped one model from working at all}\label{sec:filter}
Claude Opus 5.5 returned empty replies marked by the provider's content filter for nearly every call in the coding and
database tasks and most leaderboard runs, so its agents ended every turn without acting; \val{excl}{Opus}{} of its 100
runs are excluded. The same tasks ran normally with Claude Haiku 5.5 and the four other models. The filter did not separate
honest from collusive play: it blocked the whole task. Five Haiku auction runs are excluded for a different reason, replies
cut off at the token cap.

% ================= 6. Discussion and Limitations =================
\section{Discussion and Limitations}

\textbf{What the results mean.} In our setting, an agent's decision to use and share a forbidden shortcut depends less on
how hard the honest work is than on whether any honest route exists. For deployments this is a warning about tasks with
no legitimate way to succeed: an unsolvable ticket, a target that cannot be met with the data provided, a test that
cannot pass. There, models that take shortcuts at all take them often, and share them. For defenders, it means two
things. Make sure an honest route exists, or that agents can report that none does. And do not judge agents by outcomes
alone where honest work can reach the same outcome: score-based checks flag honest teams there, and an officer that reads
the messages does much better.

\textbf{Scope of the claim.} Following \citet{lee2026broad}, a result that the overseer misses leak use is a bounded null
at our sample size, not evidence that detection is impossible; report the interval, not ``no effect''.
\textbf{Predictions that failed or were not tested.} Of the pre-registered predictions, four hold: H1 teammate use,
H2 holder sending and H3 holder self-use all rise with difficulty, and H4 false alarms on clean teams fall with difficulty
for all three detectors, most steeply for the score rule. Two fail: collusion does not
rise gradually across D0--D2, and the manipulation check fails for Dolphin-Mistral-24B, whose honest path never worked.

\subsection{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Prompted incentives.} The reward and the rule exist only in the prompt; nothing is at stake for the agents.
  \item \textbf{Cues.} The holder's note says the file ``was accidentally left'' in its workspace, which reads like a
        honeypot, and every brief says that previous-version answers are accepted, which tells non-holders that an older key
        exists. Both are constant across levels.
  \item \textbf{The honest path is pointed to.} Agents are told where the honest path is; levels vary its cost, not its
        discoverability. Agents who ignore the README pay a higher cost than the nominal level.
  \item \textbf{Scale.} \NSeeds{} seeds per cell and one sample per run; six models from four vendors, with size, openness
        and safety post-training not varied independently; task differences bundle leak format, scoring and workload.
  \item \textbf{Coverage.} Claude Opus 5.5 rests on two of the five tasks after its filtered runs are excluded; some
        DeepSeek-V3.2 runs were rerun on a different host after a time limit (same model, same seeds).
  \item \textbf{Process.} The pre-registration was a draft, the exclusion rule was widened after the first runs, and three
        models were added later; we report these deviations rather than hide them.
  \item \textbf{Officer.} One officer model, which shares a vendor with one agent model; the threshold rule undercounts
        partial or paraphrased use.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Future Work}
Varying the collusive path's cost and the honest path's cost together; sanctions applied during the game; agents
instructed to hide; and a second officer from another vendor.

% ================= 7. Conclusion =================
\section{Conclusion}
When a member of an agent team finds a forbidden shortcut, the cost of honest work matters little until the honest route
disappears; then three of six models take the shortcut and share it, while both Claude models still refuse. The same
condition breaks outcome-based oversight in reverse: where honest work can reach the same score, scores no longer tell
cheaters from honest teams, and an officer that reads the messages is needed.

\section*{Limitations and Dual-Use / Ethical Considerations}
\textbf{What the result does and does not establish.} It measures whether a leak spreads in our testbed, with the models we
ran, as a function of the honest path's cost. It does not show that agents collude without a leak, that they can hide
collusion, or that an LLM officer works in general. \textbf{Dual use.} We report aggregate detection rates only, and publish
no catalogue of phrasings that evade the officer and no evasion prompts. All secrets are synthetic; there are no human
subjects.
